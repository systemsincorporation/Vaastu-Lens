Boundary uncertainty

Turning one way, but the dial moves the other way?

This is a different, more fundamental bug than a simple wrong number — turning right in real life should always move the dial clockwise. If it moves counter-clockwise instead (or vice versa), no offset or 180° flip can ever fix it — those only shift the reading by a fixed amount, they can't reverse which way it turns. This toggle fixes that directly.

Reading doesn't match your phone's compass?

If the dial rotates the right way as you turn, but the number itself is off by a fixed amount, this is the fix. Open your phone's own regular compass app without moving the phone, read the number it shows, and enter it here.

If your readings consistently point the opposite way from reality (e.g. facing your actual front door reads as the direction directly behind you), tap Flip 180° instead — a simpler shortcut for that one specific case.

How to find your home's directions

A compass reading is only as good as how it's taken. This covers the steps, what all 32 direction names actually mean, and how to handle a multi-floor house without re-measuring every level.

Sources for the Vastu Purusha Mandala content

The Dial tab's 32-point deity names, the Center tab's 81-pada diagram, and the "which body part of the House-God" notes throughout the app come from three manuscripts, each checked directly rather than taken on faith:

  • Brihat Samhita by Varahamihira, Adhyaya 53 ("Vastuvidya") — P.S. Sastri's 1946 English translation. This is the primary source used: its enumeration of the 45 deities, their quadrant groupings, and the limb-correspondences were read directly from the scanned text (this particular PDF has a real OCR text layer, so it could be searched).
  • Vishwakarma Vidya Prakash (Ravi Datt Shastri) — a Hindi restatement of the same material, used to cross-check: it gives the same 45 deities in the same order and the same quadrant groupings, in places almost verbatim.
  • Samarangana Sutradhara by King Bhoja (c. 1000-1055 CE) — its published synopsis of contents confirms Ch. 11 covers the 64/81/100-pada site plans and Ch. 14 is specifically about assigning deities to the House- God's limbs across those grids: the same tradition, from a third, independent manuscript. Its page images have no text layer at all (a pure scan), so unlike the other two this one could be checked visually for context but not searched or quoted directly.

Two things worth knowing plainly: this 45-deity mandala is a different, complementary layer to the familiar 8-direction deity/planet table (Kubera-north, Ishaan-northeast, Indra-east, Agni-southeast, and so on) used by the Matrix tab's room-by-room checker — the two systems are kept visually separate throughout this app rather than merged into one confusing table. And the 81-square diagram is a square grid; drawing it as 32 equal wedges around a compass circle (as this app, and the paper compass it was modeled on, both do) is a reasonable, legible alignment, not a claim that the square and the circle are mathematically identical — the deity names and which cardinal side each belongs to are solid; exact degree boundaries at the four corners are this app's own interpretation.

Modern diagnostic Vastu: the 16-zone Pancha Tattva system

Everything above is the classical 45-pada Brihat Samhita mandala. This card is a separate, more recent layer — a 16-direction system (each of the 8 primary points split in half) built around the five elements (Pancha Tattva: Water, Air/Wood, Fire, Earth, Space) that's widely taught in contemporary Vastu consulting courses. It agrees with the classical system on the broad strokes (north/water, southeast/fire, southwest/earth) but assigns life-attributes at finer resolution and drives the non-demolition remedy protocol in the next card. Treat every line here with the same "commonly cited" hedge as the rest of this app — it is not a canonical ancient text, and different modern teachers state some of these zone attributes slightly differently.

Non-demolition remedy protocol (metal strip / tape / buffer slab)

A recurring modern-diagnostic idea: instead of relocating or rebuilding a defective room, isolate its element with a thin metal strip embedded flush in the floor (or a wide colored tape as a lighter-touch alternative) around the fixture itself. The Matrix tab's Toilet and Kitchen remedy lists now include the specific metal/tape/slab called for by whichever direction you check — this card is the reference table those suggestions are drawn from.

  • Toilet isolation: cut a 3-4mm groove around 3-4 sides of the commode base and embed the zone's metal flush with the tile (sealed with epoxy/grout), or apply a 3-4 inch wide tape of the zone's color around the same base as a non-invasive alternative.
  • Kitchen burner buffer: a stone/marble slab in the buffering color (green for a hob in a Water/NE zone, yellow for a hob in a Space/NW zone) placed directly under the cooktop, reasoned as bridging the hob's own Fire element with the zone it sits in.
  • Zone area imbalance ("cut" vs "extended" zones): if one of the 16 zones occupies noticeably less floor area than the others (a "cut" zone, commonly cited threshold ~10-15% below average), a continuous light bulb or LED strip in that zone's own color, or a small two-dimensional metal pyramid plate, is a commonly cited way to "supply" the missing element without extending the building. An "extended" zone (noticeably more area than average) is instead treated with the color of the element that consumes it in the destructive cycle, or a metal divider strip at the extension's threshold.

Sourced from the same modern-diagnostic 16-zone tradition as the card above, not the classical Brihat Samhita material — the same honest caveat applies: this is what's commonly taught and cited, not a claim of settled or universally-agreed fact.

Brahmasthan scope: plot vs. garden vs. individual rooms, and multi-floor plots

Three practical questions this app gets asked often enough to answer directly:

  • Does the garden count? For the living-space Brahmasthan (the point this app's Center tab calculates), no — use only the enclosed, roofed footprint. A detached shed/berging and the open garden or driveway belong to a separate, "macro" plot-level analysis (mainly the main gate and any outdoor drainage/ water storage), not the indoor 16-zone grid used for rooms.
  • Do you calculate a separate Brahmasthan for every room? No — calculate it once for the whole enclosed footprint (the "meso" level); that single point is what determines which zone a kitchen, bedroom, or toilet falls into. A room's own center point matters only for arranging furniture inside that room (bed in the room's own SW corner, for example) — it never overrides the zone the room itself sits in relative to the whole-house Brahmasthan.
  • Multi-floor houses: if every floor shares the same outer footprint, one Brahmasthan calculation projects straight up through all of them — this is the same point covered in the multi-floor card above. If an upper floor has a different footprint (a setback terrace, a missing extension, a dormer), calculate a separate Brahmasthan for that floor's own outline using Custom or Walls entry. Traditionally, a toilet on an upper floor is flagged if it sits directly above a ground-floor kitchen stove, pooja altar, or bed headboard — worth checking floor plans against each other specifically for that, independent of each floor's own zone analysis.

Sourcing non-demolition materials in the Netherlands

The metals and tapes above are ordinary hardware-store items, not anything specialist — Gamma, Praxis, Karwei, and the Action all stock copper and aluminium tape/strip (sold for roofing/flashing or electrical work), stainless steel edge strip (sold for tiling trim), and brass strip (sold for craft/hobby use). Colored PVC/vinyl tape in the required widths is standard electrical or floor-marking tape from the same stores. A green- or yellow-toned stone slab for the kitchen-burner buffer doesn't need to be genuine Indian marble — any similarly colored granite, composite, or natural stone offcut from a Dutch kitchen showroom serves the same symbolic role in this tradition.

Two Dutch-context notes worth flagging honestly: narrow canal houses and rowhouses very often place the bathroom at the rear of the ground floor or tucked beside the stairwell for plumbing-stack reasons that have nothing to do with Vastu, so a southeast, north, or northeast toilet is common by simple layout constraint, not choice — the remedies above exist specifically for that situation. And in the damper Dutch climate, a sea-salt bowl (the northeast-toilet remedy above) will visibly clump and need refreshing faster than the "every 15 days" some sources quote — treat that as a floor, not a fixed schedule, and swap it sooner if it's clumped.

1. Calibrate first, every time you move rooms

Take off any case with a magnetic clasp or a magnetic watch/ring nearby. Move away from fridges, speakers, laptops, steel doors and window frames, and rebar-heavy concrete — these are the most common sources of a wrong reading, far more than the phone itself. Then wave the phone in a slow figure-eight for a few seconds. Do this again every time you change rooms, not just once at the start.

2. Hold it flat and pick the right spot to stand

Hold the phone flat and level, screen up, like a handheld compass. Where you stand depends on what you're measuring:

  • Main entrance direction: stand at the threshold, facing outward — the direction you'd walk if you were leaving the house.
  • A room's facing direction: stand at the center of that room, face the specific wall you're evaluating (e.g. the wall with the main window, or the wall a bed's headboard would sit against), and read the heading while facing it.
  • The whole plot's orientation: stand at the boundary, facing along the main front wall line, not diagonally across the plot — or use the Center tab's Rectangle/Custom/Camera+GPS entry instead of a single reading.

3. Take more than one reading

Take 2-3 readings a few seconds apart, recalibrating between them. Only trust them if they agree within the ± error shown under the heading number on the Dial tab. If they don't agree, something nearby is disturbing the sensor — move a step or two and try again, rather than trusting whichever number you got first.

Multi-floor houses (and Dutch rowhouses specifically)

The core idea: a building's compass orientation does not change from floor to floor. North is the same direction on the third floor as it is on the ground floor, because the floors don't rotate relative to each other — only the building's footprint (measured once) sets the orientation for every level above it. You do not need to re-measure the compass bearing on each floor separately; you only need to re-map which room sits where within that same shared footprint.

Practically: measure once, ideally outdoors at the main entrance or in the back garden (better GPS fix for true north, less indoor interference), and note that bearing as the building's front-facade direction. Every floor above inherits it. If you want a per-floor Vastu read, use the Design tab once per floor with each floor's own room layout — the room positions will differ, but they all sit inside the same compass orientation you already measured.

Dutch rowhouses (rijtjeshuizen / grachtenpanden) specifically: these are almost always a simple rectangle — a narrow street-facing front facade, a garden-facing back facade directly opposite, and two long party walls shared with the neighboring houses on either side (usually windowless, since they're shared structural walls). That means, for a typical rectangular rowhouse:

  • Front facade direction = one reading at the front door, facing the street.
  • Back facade (garden side) = that same bearing + 180°.
  • The two party walls = that same bearing ± 90°.

One outdoor reading at the front door is usually enough for the whole house, every floor included. The exception is a house that isn't a plain rectangle — an angled plot relative to the street, an L-shaped rear extension, or a dormer that changes the effective shape on upper floors. For any of those, confirm with the Center tab's Custom polygon entry (or Camera+GPS outdoors) rather than assuming the simple ±90°/180° rule holds — and if a stairwell or elevator shaft (steel) sits between rooms you're comparing, recalibrate on each side of it rather than carrying one reading past it.

All 32 compass points

The abbreviations shown throughout the app (Dial, Lens, Matrix, Design) are the standard 32-point maritime compass rose — not Vastu-specific, so there's no ambiguity in what they mean. Each of the 32 sectors is 11.25° wide. The 8 primary points (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW) each split into 4 finer points; the "by" points (like NbE, "North by East") sit exactly halfway between a primary point and the next named point toward it.
